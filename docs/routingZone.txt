RoutingZone / RoutingZoneInterconnect / RoutingZoneGrid
=======================================================

This note captures the current canonical topology model for the re-architecture.
It is intentionally direct and operational.

Companion figure:
- see `docs/routingZone-fig.txt`

Core objects
------------

1. Chip
   A chip remains a first-class object. A chip owns its identity, semantics,
   ports, and chip-local declarations such as internal wiring.

2. RoutingZone
   A RoutingZone is the atomic local routing block of the circuit world.
   A RoutingZone owns:
   - chip placement within that zone
   - chip terminal regions for the zone's major information-propagation sense
   - local intra-zone routing
   - the zone-local half of boundary traversal up to its interconnect seam

   A RoutingZone does not own chip identity globally. It owns chip placement.

3. RoutingZoneInterconnect
   A RoutingZoneInterconnect connects exactly two RoutingZones.
   It mediates continuity from one zone into the next.

   A RoutingZoneInterconnect is not a third independent routing world. It is the
   boundary mediation layer between two zones. It must know:
   - which two zones it connects
   - its placement in the grid/world
   - the relevant boundary channels
   - how wires exit one zone and are picked up by the next

4. RoutingZoneGrid
   RoutingZoneGrid is the world topology. It is the first global constraint.
   Zones are placed in a 2D grid. Interconnects are placed between neighboring
   zones. Most initial examples reduce to a 1 x N grid, but the model is 2D.

   RoutingZoneGrid owns:
   - world topology
   - zone positions
   - interconnect positions
   - macro route-path selection across multiple zones
   - advance reservation of interconnect capacity classes for long-haul traffic

Ownership summary
-----------------

- Chips are first-class objects.
- Chips belong to exactly one RoutingZone.
- RoutingZones own chip placement, not chip identity.
- RoutingZoneInterconnects connect exactly two RoutingZones.
- RoutingZoneGrid owns the world topology and macro route-path planning.

Current simple regime
---------------------

The current visual semantic begins from propagation depth plus major sense.

For a West-to-East world:
- a call structure of depth N induces a RoutingZoneGrid of 1 x (N - 1)
- chip assignment follows propagation depth

Example: three function layers deep
-----------------------------------

Assume a horizontal West-to-East world with two RoutingZones:

- RoutingZone [1,1]
- RoutingZone [2,1]

Placement:

- layer 0 root chip:
  West-ChipTerminal of RoutingZone [1,1]

- layer 1 children:
  East-ChipTerminal of RoutingZone [1,1]

- layer 2 children:
  West-ChipTerminal of RoutingZone [2,1]

Routing:

1. Inside RoutingZone [1,1]
   Connectivity between layer 0 and layer 1 is solved as an intra-zone problem.

2. Between RoutingZone [1,1] and RoutingZone [2,1]
   Connectivity between layer 1 and layer 2 is solved across the interconnect
   seam. RoutingZone [1,1] owns the outbound half from its East-ChipTerminal,
   and RoutingZone [2,1] owns the inbound half into its West-ChipTerminal.

This means continuity is shared across adjacent zones. Wires must exit one zone
at explicit locations so the next zone can pick them up at the same locations.

Important constraint
--------------------

No chip may appear in more than one RoutingZone.

That rule is deliberate. It prevents placement ambiguity and keeps the topology
disciplined. A chip participates in the world through the RoutingZone that owns
its placement.

Chip solving before zone solving
--------------------------------

Chip-local routing must be solved first.

Reason:
- chip-local routing fixes the chip's intrinsic geometry
- zone-local placement must then size chip terminal regions to accommodate the
  chips assigned to that zone

So the layered order is:
- solve chip internals
- derive solved chip geometry
- place chips in RoutingZones
- solve zone-local routing

Tiered solve order
------------------

The intended solve is tiered.

1. RoutingZoneGrid pre-analysis
   Determine whether any route must cross more than one RoutingZone.
   Reserve interconnect capacity classes a priori.

   These reservations are capacity reservations, not specific-wire assignments.

2. RoutingZone local solve
   Each RoutingZone solves all of its local connectivity in batch, using solved
   chips and respecting the world-level reservations.

3. RoutingZoneInterconnect local seam solve
   Each interconnect solves the local cross-zone continuity between its two
   neighboring zones.

4. RoutingZoneGrid cross-grid finalize
   RoutingZoneGrid lays the longer cross-grid connections through the reserved
   path, with interplay from the local zone and interconnect results.

Long-haul traffic
-----------------

Routing may cross many zones and many interconnects.

For example, if a chip in RoutingZone [3,3] must connect to a chip in [1,1],
RoutingZoneGrid must know the world topology and choose a macro path such as:

- [3,3] -> [2,3] -> [1,3] -> [1,2] -> [1,1]

assuming the current world policy disallows unnecessary zig-zag.

RoutingZoneGrid chooses the macro route sequence first. RoutingZones and
RoutingZoneInterconnects then realize their local segments under that world-level
constraint.

Sense and placement
-------------------

Chip placement inside a RoutingZone is relative to the zone's information
propagation sense.

That means chip placement is constrained to named terminal regions implied by the
major sense:
- West/East for a West-to-East zone
- North/South for a North-to-South zone

Interconnect orientation is derived from the two connected zone positions in the
simple case. Routing sense still remains explicit, for example clockwise or
anti-clockwise travel through longitude/latitude channels.

What this means architecturally
-------------------------------

This model changes the topological unit of the new engine.

The canonical solved unit is no longer "a placed chip lane" or a separate
global layout pass by itself. The canonical local unit is RoutingZone, and the
canonical world unit is RoutingZoneGrid.

Long-term chip placement must become subordinate to RoutingZone ownership
rather than the other way around.
